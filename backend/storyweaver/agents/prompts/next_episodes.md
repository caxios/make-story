## How to plan them

**A run, not a list.** These {count} episodes are read one after another. Each
follows on from the one before it — consequences, open questions, the mood it
ended on — and hands something to the next. Episode {start} follows on from
episode {previous}.

**Paced for where they sit.** Read the section on where these episodes sit.
Stay inside the job of the part (or parts) they fall in; if the part runs on
past episode {end}, the part is not finished by {end}. Do not start what a
later part is for, and do not approach the ending while most of the book is
still ahead. A serial moves in small steps: a discovery, a decision, a new face,
a first crack, a reversal — with a bigger turn every so often, not every episode.

**Put the planned beats where they are planned.** If the layout plants a thread
at episode N, plant it in or near episode N. If it pays one off at N, pay it off
there, and not before. Threads planned for later are kept alive — reminded,
deepened — and not answered. The same for relationships: make each planned
turning point happen around its episode, show the relationship moving in small
ways in between, and do not bring a later turn forward.

**The whole cast, not only the lead.** Across these episodes, give the
supporting characters their own moments and let their relationships with each
other move, not only their relationship with the protagonist. Use what the cast
list says each of them wants and hides. Rotate focus; a stretch of twenty
episodes where most of the cast never appears has wasted it.

**Stay inside what exists.** Use the cast and the world as written above. Do
not introduce a new major character or break a rule. A minor new face or a new
place is fine when an episode needs one.

**The author's direction wins.** If the author said what they want from these
episodes, that is what they are about — fit everything else around it.

## What to write

For each episode from {start} to {end}, in order and with exactly those numbers:
one paragraph of three to five sentences, in Korean unless the story so far is
in another language — who is in it, what happens, what changes, and the beat it
ends on.

These are plans, not prose. No dialogue, no headings, no lists, no titles, no
commentary.
