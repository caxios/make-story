## What to produce

**Parts.** Divide episodes 1 to {target} into consecutive parts (1부, 2부, …).
Choose the number of parts the story needs — a 40-episode work may have two or
three, a 400-episode work usually five to ten. Parts must cover every episode
from 1 to {target} with no gaps and no overlap. For each part give a short
title and a purpose: what happens in it, what changes, and where the story must
stand when the part ends. {fixed}

**Pace it for this length.** A web novel of {target} episodes is read one
episode at a time over a long stretch; each episode is a small step. The
opening part sets up the protagonist, the world and the first conflict slowly
enough that a reader settles in. The central conflict escalates across the
middle parts in stages — each part raises the stakes or changes the situation,
it does not repeat the previous one. The ending as written in the concept is
reached only in the final part, near episode {target}. Nothing that belongs in a
late part is spent in an early one.

**Threads (떡밥).** Plan the questions and promises that carry a reader across
this length: mysteries, secrets, rivalries, prophecies, hidden identities,
debts. For each give the episode around which it is first planted and the
episode around which it is paid off, and a note of what it is and how it is
answered (this note is for the author, so include the answer). Spread them: some
short threads paid off within a part, some long ones planted early and paid off
parts later, and the biggest ones reserved for the final parts. A {target}-episode
work needs enough of them that no stretch of the story runs without an open
question — roughly one for every 15 to 30 episodes. Use the concept's secrets
and the characters' hidden goals as material.

**Relationships.** A serial runs on what is between its people, so plan that
too. Lay out how the cast's relationships develop across the whole length:
alliances that form and fracture, rivals who come round or go darker, a romance
that takes its time, a mentor who fails, family who are the problem. Every
major character should be tied to at least two others by something that
changes; supporting characters get at least one. For each relationship give the
two characters (names exactly as the work spells them), where it starts, where
it ends up, and its turning points — the episodes around which it shifts, one
sentence each. Space the turning points across the length like the threads:
tie them to the parts and to the threads where they meet, and keep the
decisive turns for late parts. For a large cast, cover every major pairing
rather than a few.

Stay inside the concept: its cast, its world and its ending. Do not change the
ending or invent a new main character; supporting characters and places may be
implied by a part's purpose where the story needs them.
